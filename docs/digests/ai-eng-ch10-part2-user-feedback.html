User Feedback: Extracting, Designing and Handling the Limits of Conversational Feedback

Part 1 built the system. This part is about the signal that tells you whether it's working and, just as importantly, supplies the data to make it better: user feedback. In AI products the conversational interface makes feedback easier for users to give and harder for developers to read, because it's tangled up in everyday dialogue. We'll cover how to extract feedback from conversations (natural language signals and behavioral ones), how to design when and how to ask for it, and the biases and feedback loops that can quietly wreck a product. The chapter's Summary wraps up both halves.

In one line

User feedback, explicit or buried in how people talk to and act around your app, is proprietary data that powers evaluation, model improvement and personalization, but it's biased, noisy and capable of steering your product somewhere you don't want it to go.

The big idea

Feedback has always guided software, but for AI applications it has a second job: it's a data source for improving models. The chapter ties it back to the data flywheel from Chapter 8. A product that launches quickly, attracts users and collects good feedback builds a stock of proprietary data that competitors find hard to match. Two cautions run through the section. First, feedback is user data, so the same care applies as for any data: respect privacy and tell users how it's used. Second, feedback is incomplete and biased, so using it indiscriminately can reinforce biases, narrow the product, or even train a model to flatter users instead of being accurate.

Key terms

  • Explicit feedback: what users deliberately give when asked: thumbs up/down, star ratings, yes/no on "did we solve your problem?".
  • Implicit feedback: signals inferred from what users do (buying a recommended product). Highly application-specific.
  • Conversational feedback: feedback expressed through the conversation itself, in message content and in patterns of behavior.
  • Natural language feedback: signals in the content of messages (early termination, error corrections, complaints, sentiment).
  • Preference data: (query, winning response, losing response), used to align a model with human preference.
  • Regeneration: asking the app to produce another response.
  • Comparative feedback: users choosing between two options.
  • Inpainting: selecting part of a generated image and prompting a fix for just that part (an example of human-AI collaboration).
  • Data donation flow: asking users to share their recent interaction data along with their feedback.
  • Leniency, position, preference and recency bias: ways feedback skews (explained below).
  • Degenerate feedback loop: predictions influence feedback, which influences the next model, amplifying initial biases (also called exposure, popularity bias or filter bubbles).
  • Sycophancy: a model's tendency to tell users what they seem to want to hear.

How it works

Grounding: why feedback matters more for AI

Feedback does two classic jobs: it evaluates how the app is doing and informs development. For AI apps it also personalizes models for individual users and trains future model versions. That's why a well-designed feedback system is described as necessary to create the data flywheel. The chapter notes that, traditionally, feedback design was seen as a product responsibility, but because it now supplies training data, AI engineers are getting involved, which fits the book's early theme that AI engineering is moving closer to product. (A footnote also observes that open source applications make feedback harder to collect, because you don't know how people use them.)

Explicit vs. implicit feedback

Explicit feedback answers a direct request and is fairly standard and well understood across apps. It's easier to interpret but demands user effort, so it tends to be sparse, especially for apps with small user bases, and it suffers from response biases (unhappy users may be more likely to complain, making feedback look more negative than reality). Implicit feedback is far more abundant (limited, the chapter says, only by your imagination) but noisier and harder to interpret. Foundation models create new genres of implicit feedback, mostly through conversation.

The chapter's trip-planning example shows how content carries feedback. After an assistant suggests three Sydney hotels, "Yes, book me the one close to galleries" reveals an interest in art, while "Is there nothing under $200?" reveals price sensitivity and suggests the assistant hasn't understood you yet. Feedback extracted this way can serve three purposes: evaluation (metrics to monitor), development (training or guiding future models) and personalization. It can be inferred from both what users say and how they behave. The chapter notes intuition about cues is a start, but rigorous data analysis and user studies are needed to understand them.

Natural language feedback signals

  • Early termination. The user stops a generation halfway, exits the app, tells a voice assistant to stop, or leaves an agent hanging without answering. Likely the conversation isn't going well.
  • Error correction. Follow-ups starting with "No, ..." or "I meant, ...", or a rephrased request (detectable with heuristics or ML models), suggest the response missed. Users may point out specifics ("Bill is the suspect, not the victim") or nudge an agent toward other actions ("You should also check the company's GitHub page"). They might ask for confirmation ("Are you sure?", "Check again", "Show me the sources"), which doesn't necessarily mean the answer was wrong, but may mean it lacked detail or that trust is low.
  • Edits. When an app lets users edit generated output directly (such as code), an edit is a very strong signal the original wasn't right. It's also a ready-made preference example: the original is the losing response and the edited version is the winning one.
  • Complaints. Users may say the answer is wrong, irrelevant, toxic, too long or lacking detail. The chapter reproduces a table of eight feedback types obtained by clustering the FITS dataset (the top groups were users clarifying their demand again at roughly 27%, complaints that the bot didn't answer or gave irrelevant information at roughly 16%, and pointing to specific search results that could answer the question, also roughly 16%). Knowing how the bot fails lets you act, for instance by changing the prompt to be more concise or more specific.
  • Sentiment. General frustration ("Uggh") without a reason. Tracking sentiment across a conversation can show how the bot is doing; the chapter notes call centers track rising voice volume, and that someone who starts angry and ends happy suggests the issue was resolved.
  • Model refusals. The model's own responses carry signal too: "Sorry, I don't know that one" or "As a language model, I can't..." likely means an unhappy user.

Other conversational feedback (behavior, not words)

  • Regeneration. Asking for another answer may mean dissatisfaction, or just wanting options (common with creative work). The signal may be stronger under usage-based billing, where users are less likely to regenerate out of idle curiosity. The chapter's author regenerates complex requests to check consistency; two contradicting answers mean neither is trusted. Apps can follow up by asking whether the new response is better or worse, giving comparison data usable for preference finetuning.
  • Conversation organization. Deleting (a strong negative signal, unless the user is hiding something embarrassing), renaming (probably good conversation, bad auto-title), sharing, bookmarking.
  • Conversation length. Positive or negative depending on the app: for an AI companion, long may mean enjoyment; for customer support, long may mean the bot is inefficient.
  • Dialogue diversity (distinct token or topic counts). A long conversation with repeated lines may mean the user is stuck in a loop.

Interpretation is hard. Sharing, for example, can be positive or negative: the chapter describes one friend who shares conversations when the model makes glaring mistakes and another who shares useful ones with coworkers. So study why your users do things, and combine signals (a rephrase after a share suggests the conversation fell short).

Feedback design: when to collect

Feedback can be collected throughout the user journey, but collection should be non-intrusive. Valuable moments:

  • At the beginning (calibration). Face ID must scan your face; a voice assistant asks you to read a sentence; a language app gauges your level. Calibration is necessary for some apps but should be optional for others since it creates friction; fall back to neutral and calibrate over time.
  • When something bad happens. Hallucinations, blocked legitimate requests, bad images, slow responses. Offer downvote, regenerate, switch model, or just let users say "You're wrong" or "I want something shorter". Ideally users can still finish their task: let them edit (change a wrongly assigned category), collaborate with the AI (inpainting a region of an image), or hand off to a human (many support bots do when a conversation drags or users seem frustrated).
  • When the model has low confidence. If it can't tell what the user wants (a short vs. a section-by-section summary), show both and let the user choose, provided it doesn't add latency. Side-by-side comparisons produce preference data. Showing two full responses is explicit feedback that users may not have time to read carefully, which makes votes noisy; some apps show only the opening of each and let users expand one, and the chapter says it's unclear which design gives more reliable feedback. Another example: a photo app asking "are these the same person?" when unsure.
  • When something good happens? Thumbs up, favoriting, sharing. Apple's human interface guidance, as the chapter cites it, warns against asking for both positive and negative feedback, since your app should produce good results by default and asking can imply good results are exceptions. But a product manager the chapter quotes wants positive feedback because it shows which features users love, helping the team focus. A mitigation for clutter: ask only a small share of users at a time (for example 1% of a large base), keeping in mind that asking fewer users raises the risk of feedback bias.

Feedback design: how to collect

Feedback should fit into the workflow, be easy to ignore, and ideally come with incentives. The chapter's examples and rules:

  • Midjourney's workflow. Each prompt yields four images and the user can upscale one, make variations of one, or regenerate. Upscaling is the strongest positive signal for the chosen image, variations a weaker positive signal, regenerate suggests none were good (though users may regenerate just to see more).
  • GitHub Copilot. Suggestions appear in lighter text; Tab accepts, continuing to type ignores. Both are feedback with zero extra effort.
  • Integrated vs. standalone. Products embedded in a workflow (Gmail drafting an email) can see how output is used or edited; a standalone chat app generally can't tell whether the email it wrote was ever sent.
  • Context. A thumbs-down alone is useful for counting; deeper analysis needs context such as the previous 5 to 10 turns, which may contain PII and so needs consent. Options: terms of service that allow analytics and improvement, or a data donation flow (a checkbox to share recent data with the feedback).
  • Explain how feedback is used (personalization, general statistics, model training) and, only where true, reassure users their data won't train models or leave their device.
  • Don't ask the impossible. Comparing two answers to a statistics question the user can't judge is pointless; offer an "I don't know" option. For math-type questions the right answer isn't a matter of preference.
  • Avoid confusing design. The author's anecdote: a feedback form put an angry emoji, mapped to one star, where five stars should have been, so positive reviews arrived as 1/5. Use clear icons and tooltips.
  • Private or public? Users are often more candid in private; the chapter notes X made likes private in 2024 and its owner claimed a significant increase in likes. But private signals reduce discoverability and explainability (you can't see what your connections liked, and may not understand why a post appears in your feed).

Feedback limitations

The chapter's blunt line: feedback isn't a free lunch. Know the biases and design around them.

  • Leniency bias. People rate more positively than warranted, to avoid conflict or effort (if a low rating triggers a follow-up form, they pick positive to be done). The chapter's example: Uber drivers' average rating was 4.8 in 2015 with scores below 4.6 risking deactivation. That is not necessarily fatal, since the goal is separating good from bad drivers, but check your rating distribution, and consider replacing numbers with descriptive options that remove the stigma of low scores (the chapter offers unvalidated example wordings from "Great ride" to "Don't match me with this driver again").
  • Randomness. Low-motivation users click arbitrarily, for example choosing at random between two long answers they didn't read.
  • Position bias. The first option gets more clicks regardless of quality. Mitigate by randomizing positions or modeling the true success rate given position.
  • Preference biases. People may prefer the longer answer even if less accurate, or favor whichever they saw last (recency bias).

Degenerate feedback loops. You only get feedback on what you show. When predictions influence feedback, which then influences the next model, initial biases amplify. A video that ranks slightly higher gets more clicks, so it's boosted further, and a similar-quality video never gets a chance (popularity or exposure bias, filter bubbles). The chapter's cat-photo example: a few users like cats, the system makes more cat photos, attracts cat lovers, and soon the product is a cat haven. The same mechanism can amplify more harmful biases. The chapter also cites studies that training on human feedback can push models toward sycophancy: telling users what they seem to want, even when it isn't accurate. Its conclusion: understand feedback's limits and effects before building it into the product, because used indiscriminately it can perpetuate biases and "destroy your product".

Why it matters

If you can only do one thing after reading this part, instrument your app so ordinary user actions (accept, edit, regenerate, stop, rephrase) are recorded as signals, and then read them skeptically. The data flywheel from Chapter 8 depends on it, and so do your evaluation metrics, your monitoring and the preference data for future finetuning. Just as important is knowing how these signals mislead.

Remember this

  • Explicit feedback is clear but sparse and biased; implicit feedback is abundant but noisy and application-specific.
  • Conversations contain signals: early termination, corrections and rephrasing, edits (which double as preference pairs), complaints, sentiment, regeneration, and how people organize or share chats.
  • Ask at the right moments (start, failure, low confidence) in a way that fits the workflow; explain how feedback is used and get consent for context.
  • Watch for leniency, randomness, position and preference biases; randomize positions where you can.
  • Degenerate feedback loops and sycophancy mean feedback should not be applied blindly.

Try it yourself

  1. Signal detector (about an hour). Write a small classifier using regular expressions that flags error-correction ("No, ...", "I meant ...") and reconfirmation ("Are you sure", "Check again", "Show me the sources") in user messages. Test it on 15 invented messages, including a few tricky ones such as a message that begins with "Nobody...". Done when you've found at least one false positive or miss and written how you'd improve it. (A basic version I ran flagged the right cases on my four sample messages.)
  2. Edit-to-preference converter. Given pairs of (original model output, user-edited output) for a prompt, produce records of the form {"prompt", "winning", "losing"} and write them to a JSON lines file, skipping pairs where the edit is trivial (for example only whitespace). Done when you can produce 10 records from sample data and explain one reason an edit might not mean the original was bad.
  3. Position bias simulator. Simulate two items of equal true quality shown in random order, where the item in slot 2 is noticed less. Count click rates by slot over 10,000 trials. Done when you see the slot-1 rate exceed slot-2 even though quality is equal. (My toy run gave about 0.25 vs. 0.15 with the assumptions I chose; the numbers are artifacts of the made-up model.) Then add a correction that divides clicks by a per-slot weight and check the gap closes.
  4. Rating distribution check. Make a list of 200 star ratings with most at 5 and a few at 4 and lower (invent it). Compute the histogram and mean, then rewrite the five scale labels as descriptive phrases as in the chapter's idea. Done when you can explain what the distribution suggests about leniency and what you'd change about the question.
  5. Feedback loop toy. Two items, A and B, with equal true appeal. Each round the system shows each item with probability proportional to its past clicks plus one. Run 1,000 rounds from several random seeds. Done when you can show that the final shares often drift far from 50/50 across seeds, then add 10% random exploration and compare. This is a toy, not a model of any real recommender.
  6. Feedback UX review. Pick two AI products you use and list every place they collect feedback, whether explicit or implicit. For each, note when it's asked, how much effort it takes, and what bias it's most exposed to. Done when you can propose one change that gets a cleaner signal without adding user effort.
